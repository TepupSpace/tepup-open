TepUp
Tép Riu Stép Up Stép Out: where commoners can safely learn and freely share their knowledge.

1. Organisational Background
Mission and activities
TepUp is a Vietnamese civic literacy platform built on the conviction that civic knowledge is not an ideological project but a practical life skill. Our mission is to make foundational civic knowledge accessible, personal, and impossible to ignore for young Vietnamese people, by building free, open-source, interactive learning experiences grounded in everyday Vietnamese life and law.
Our vision is a Vietnam in which any person, regardless of location, income, or educational background, can understand their rights and obligations, ask informed questions of public institutions, and participate meaningfully in civic life. We take our intellectual inspirations from John S. Mill’s proposition that liberty cannot exist without the plurality of ideas and experiences, from Phan Chau Trinh's khai dân trí, the conviction that enlightening the public mind must precede any meaningful social change, from Habermas's understanding of the public sphere as a communicative space where citizens move from passive consumers of information to active, self-directed participants in life, and from Nancy Fraser’s subaltern counterpublics, parallel discursive arenas where members of subordinated groups invent and circulate counter‑discourses.
TepUp operates as a small, volunteer-driven team based across Vietnam and the diaspora. Our core activities are:
Developing interactive civic literacy courses in Vietnamese, using a modular block-based architecture, comparable to platforms like Brilliant, that combines storytelling, gamification, and bite-size learning to make complex civic concepts personally relevant. Our three courses cover taxation (Thuế & Ta), critical thinking (Tư Duy & Ta), and digital privacy (Quyền Số & Ta). Together they trace a progression from understanding what the state does with your money, to thinking clearly about what you are told, to protecting yourself in the digital spaces you already inhabit.
Building a community knowledge creation framework through which Vietnamese lawyers, educators, journalists, practitioners and community members can contribute their own courses and knowledge to the platform without technical expertise, transforming TepUp from a content provider into a civic knowledge commons.
Maintaining a censorship-resistant technical infrastructure across multiple domains and hosting layers, with privacy-preserving analytics, anonymous learning by default, and fully open-source, portable content, ensuring the platform remains accessible regardless of blocking attempts.
TepUp is distinct from the journalism and advocacy platforms that have historically been blocked or shut down in Vietnam. We do not publish political commentary or human rights documentation. We introduce civic knowledge as practical self-protection rather than dissent. This is a principled strategic choice: a platform that cannot be accessed cannot educate anyone.
The platform is built using AI-assisted development tools (Claude Code, Vercel) to compensate for limited in-house technical capacity, consistent with our broader principle that tools previously requiring specialist expertise should be available to everyone. All civic content is human-reviewed before publication.
TepUp is currently live at tepup.space, with a prototype taxation module and growing contributor community. This funding will enable us to complete the full first course, launch the contributor framework, and establish the platform's long-term sustainability.

2. Project Details
2.1 Problem Analysis
Vietnam's young population, over 60% under 35, is among the most digitally connected in Southeast Asia, yet remains among the most politically quiescent. This is not a product of apathy. A recent research documents that Vietnamese youth inhabit a structurally engineered incentive environment in which the perceived costs of civic engagement are deliberately inflated and its perceived benefits systematically dampened. Repression works less through mass punishment and more through calculated uncertainty: occasional, visible arrests paired with vague laws create a chilling effect far larger than the actual number of people targeted. Simultaneously, state narratives of national progress frame civic questioning as pointless or disloyal. The result is what the researcher calls "a structurally produced equilibrium of political restraint amid cultural dynamism": young Vietnamese redirect energy toward non-contentious activities, leaving civic knowledge severely underdeveloped.
This quiescence is compounded by a deep civic literacy deficit. Most Vietnamese adults have little working knowledge of how public institutions function, how taxes are collected and spent, or what rights they hold under domestic law. State-controlled education and mass media have long produced passive consumers rather than engaged citizens. Modern algorithmic platforms accelerate this further, narrowing the diversity of ideas that constitutes genuine civic formation.
The collapse of Vietnam's independent civic information ecosystem makes this concrete. Platforms with explicit civic aims were consistently firewall-blocked or shut down: Talawas (blocked 2004, closed 2010), the Diễn đàn Xã hội Dân sự (shut down completely after 2013), and today sites including TinhThanKhaiMinh.org, Project88, and the BBC remain inaccessible without a VPN. Vietnam blocked over 2,705 websites in 2022, and its Decree 147 in 2024 prevents social media users from engaging in independent journalism or spreading information about government wrongdoing. Luật Khoa Tạp Chí, one of the last independent Vietnamese legal outlets, operates in exile, unreachable domestically without circumvention tools.
The consequences extend beyond any individual platform. Without a foundation of civic literacy, an understanding of the social contract, of rights and obligations, of how public resources are allocated and on whose authority, citizens cannot meaningfully participate in the informal opinion-formation processes that, even under constrained political conditions, constitute pressure for accountability. Phan Chau Trinh understood this over a century ago: khai dân trí must come first. A population that does not understand taxation cannot ask where public money goes. A population that cannot identify a logical fallacy cannot recognise manipulation. A population unaware of digital surveillance cannot protect itself from it. Without the intellectual infrastructure of civic engagement, the path to accountability remains closed.
What the history of platform closures reveals is a critical structural gap: the platforms that were shut down were primarily journalism and advocacy platforms; they told citizens what was happening and why it was wrong. What has not existed is a civic literacy infrastructure, a space where people learn the foundational concepts that allow them to make sense of what they receive, ask meaningful questions of public institutions, and understand their own position in the social contract. TepUp is designed to fill this gap, and to remain accessible by operating in the space that advocacy platforms do not: practical, personal, non-confrontational civic literacy. In building an infrastructure not through confrontation but through curiosity, not through advocacy but through learning that makes the practical, lived relevance of civic knowledge impossible to ignore, it exists to begin rebuilding what decades of platform closures and civic silence have left bare.
